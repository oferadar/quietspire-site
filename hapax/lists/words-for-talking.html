Words for talking

Words for every way a person can talk: too much, too loudly, too grandly, too little, or exactly right.

concise

kuhn-SAHYS, adjective

Saying much clearly in few words.

“His concise summary saved the meeting twenty minutes.”

grumble

GRUHM-buhl, verb

To complain in a low, muttering way.

“He grumbled about the early meeting, then brought donuts anyway.”

mumble

MUHM-buhl, verb

To speak quietly and unclearly.

“She mumbled an apology into her scarf.”

articulate

ahr-TIK-yuh-lit, adjective

Expressing ideas clearly and fluently.

“The most articulate answer came from the quietest student.”

bombastic

bom-BAS-tik, adjective

High-sounding and inflated, with little substance.

“The keynote was bombastic; the release notes told the truth.”

eloquent

EL-uh-kwuhnt, adjective

Fluent, forceful, and persuasive in speaking or writing.

“His eloquent toast left half the wedding in tears.”

circumlocution

sur-kuhm-loh-KYOO-shuhn, noun

The use of many words where fewer would do, often to be evasive.

“His answer was pure circumlocution, five minutes of talk without a yes or no.”

garrulous

GAR-uh-luhs, adjective

Excessively talkative.

“The garrulous cab driver covered politics, soup, and his cousin’s wedding in one ride.”

laconic

luh-KON-ik, adjective

Using very few words; terse to the point of seeming curt.

“His laconic reply of ‘It works’ closed a three-week email thread.”

loquacious

loh-KWAY-shuhs, adjective

Very talkative.

“Two loquacious hosts, one hour, zero finished sentences.”

raconteur

rak-on-TUR, noun

A skilled teller of stories.

“Every family reunion runs on one raconteur and one long table.”

reticent

RET-ih-suhnt, adjective

Reluctant to speak or reveal one’s thoughts.

“The founder stayed reticent about the acquisition price.”

sesquipedalian

ses-kwih-puh-DAYL-yuhn, adjective

Given to using long words.

“His sesquipedalian emails required a second reading and a dictionary.”

taciturn

TAS-ih-turn, adjective

Habitually quiet and reserved in speech.

“Her taciturn grandfather said more with a raised eyebrow than most do in a speech.”

vociferous

voh-SIF-er-uhs, adjective

Expressing opinions loudly and forcefully.

“The rezoning proposal met vociferous opposition at the town meeting.”

badinage

bad-ih-NAHZH, noun

Light, playful back-and-forth talk.

“Their meetings open with ten minutes of badinage, then budgets.”

deipnosophist

dyp-NOS-uh-fist, noun

A masterful dinner-table conversationalist.

“Seat the deipnosophist mid-table and the party runs itself.”

expatiate

ek-SPAY-shee-ayt, verb

To speak or write at great length on a subject.

“Ask about sourdough and he will expatiate until the loaf cools.”

grandiloquent

gran-DIL-uh-kwuhnt, adjective

Pompous or extravagant in language or style, meant to impress.

“The menu’s grandiloquent description amounted to, essentially, toast.”

logomachy

loh-GOM-uh-kee, noun

A dispute about words rather than substance.

“The standards meeting collapsed into logomachy over ‘shall’ and ‘should.’”

orotund

OR-uh-tund, adjective

Full and resonant in voice; pompously grand in style.

“The announcer’s orotund delivery made bingo sound like opera.”

pleonasm

PLEE-uh-naz-uhm, noun

The use of more words than necessary.

“‘Free gift’ is the pleonasm marketing cannot quit.”

rodomontade

rod-uh-mon-TAYD, noun

Boastful, arrogant talk.

“The pitch deck was forty slides of rodomontade and one number.”

stentorian

sten-TOR-ee-uhn, adjective

Extremely loud and powerful in voice.

“The coach’s stentorian whisper carried three fields over.”
