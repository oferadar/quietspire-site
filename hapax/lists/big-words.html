Big words worth using

A long word is only worth using when it says something a short one cannot. Every word here names an idea that would otherwise take a whole sentence. Sesquipedalian is included for anyone overdoing it.

ambivalent

am-BIV-uh-luhnt, adjective

Having mixed or contradictory feelings about something.

“He felt ambivalent about the promotion: more money, but far less time at home.”

anomaly

uh-NOM-uh-lee, noun

Something that deviates from what is normal.

“One warm week in January is an anomaly, not a season.”

arbitrary

AHR-bih-trair-ee, adjective

Based on whim rather than reason or rule.

“The 9 p.m. cutoff seemed arbitrary until they explained the shift change.”

catalyst

KAT-l-ist, noun

Something that triggers change.

“The blackout became the catalyst for the whole grid upgrade.”

conflate

kuhn-FLAYT, verb

To mistakenly merge two distinct things into one.

“The report conflates users who left with users who never signed up.”

corroborate

kuh-ROB-uh-rayt, verb

To confirm with supporting evidence.

“Two receipts corroborated her account of the timeline.”

eclectic

ih-KLEK-tik, adjective

Drawing from many diverse sources.

“Her eclectic playlist ran from opera to garage punk.”

egregious

ih-GREE-juhs, adjective

Outstandingly bad; shocking in its degree.

“An egregious typo shipped on the billboard itself.”

empirical

em-PIR-i-kuhl, adjective

Based on observation rather than theory.

“The empirical answer, because they tested it, ended the argument.”

epitome

ih-PIT-uh-mee, noun

A perfect example of a quality or type.

“The corner diner is the epitome of unhurried breakfast.”

exacerbate

ig-ZAS-er-bayt, verb

To make a problem worse.

“Honking exacerbates precisely nothing.”

juxtapose

JUHK-stuh-pohz, verb

To place side by side for contrast.

“The exhibit juxtaposes drone photos with hand-drawn maps.”

mitigate

MIT-ih-gayt, verb

To make less severe or harmful.

“Sandbags mitigated the worst of the flooding.”

pragmatic

prag-MAT-ik, adjective

Dealing with things in a practical way rather than by theory or ideals.

“We took the pragmatic route and shipped a simpler version first.”

quintessential

kwin-tuh-SEN-shuhl, adjective

Representing the most perfect example of a quality or type.

“The corner diner is the quintessential American restaurant: coffee, counter, and pie.”

ubiquitous

yoo-BIK-wih-tuhs, adjective

Present or found everywhere at once.

“Smartphones are now so ubiquitous that payphones read as museum pieces.”

ameliorate

uh-MEEL-yuh-rayt, verb

To make a bad situation better.

“Small raises did little to ameliorate the staffing crisis.”

anachronistic

uh-nak-ruh-NIS-tik, adjective

Belonging to a different time period; conspicuously out of date.

“The fax machine felt anachronistic in an office run entirely on chat apps.”

apocryphal

uh-POK-ruh-fuhl, adjective

Of doubtful authenticity, although widely circulated as true.

“The story about the CEO’s garage prototype is almost certainly apocryphal.”

cogent

KOH-juhnt, adjective

Clear, logical, and convincing.

“One cogent paragraph beat forty slides.”

dichotomy

dahy-KOT-uh-mee, noun

A division into two opposed parts.

“The old dichotomy of work and life dissolves when you love the work.”

equivocate

ih-KWIV-uh-kayt, verb

To use vague language to avoid committing oneself.

“Asked for a delivery date, the vendor equivocated for a week.”

esoteric

es-uh-TER-ik, adjective

Understood by only a small, specialized group.

“The debate turned on an esoteric point of maritime law.”

hegemony

hi-JEM-uh-nee, noun

Dominance of one group over others.

“The platform’s hegemony over search shaped a decade of the web.”

idiosyncratic

id-ee-oh-sin-KRAT-ik, adjective

Peculiar to one individual; distinctively personal.

“His idiosyncratic filing system worked for him alone.”

obfuscate

OB-fuh-skayt, verb

To deliberately make something unclear or difficult to understand.

“The report seemed designed to obfuscate rather than explain the losses.”

ostensible

ah-STEN-suh-bl, adjective

Stated as true, though perhaps not actually so.

“The ostensible reason was scheduling; the real one was budget.”

quixotic

kwik-SOT-ik, adjective

Extremely idealistic to the point of being unrealistic or impractical.

“His quixotic plan to bike across three continents somehow succeeded.”

sesquipedalian

ses-kwih-puh-DAYL-yuhn, adjective

Given to using long words.

“His sesquipedalian emails required a second reading and a dictionary.”

verisimilitude

vair-ih-sih-MIL-ih-tood, noun

The appearance of being true or real.

“The film’s verisimilitude came from casting actual fishermen instead of actors.”
