Negative words to describe a person

Words for difficult people, sharper than rude or mean: the flatterer, the liar, the one who will not be told, and the one who will not stop talking.

cynical

SIN-i-kuhl, adjective

Believing people act mainly from self-interest.

“Years in sales had made him cynical about limited-time offers.”

gruff

GRUHF, adjective

Rough or stern in manner or voice.

“The mechanic’s gruff reply hid a genuine kindness.”

reckless

REK-lis, adjective

Careless about danger or consequences.

“One reckless lane change undid twenty careful miles.”

stubborn

STUHB-ern, adjective

Refusing to change one’s mind or course.

“The stubborn stain outlasted three washes and a lecture.”

aloof

uh-LOOF, adjective

Cool and distant in manner; not friendly or forthcoming.

“The new manager seemed aloof at first, but she was simply observing.”

complacent

kuhm-PLAY-suhnt, adjective

Self-satisfied and unaware of danger.

“Three good quarters made the team complacent by the fourth.”

Changing loyalties, interests, or affection often.

“Fashion is fickle; last year’s must-have is this year’s donation pile.”

Wicked, villainous, or criminal.

“The plot turned on a nefarious scheme to rig the raffle.”

negligent

NEG-li-juhnt, adjective

Failing to take proper care.

“The audit found the firm negligent in its record keeping.”

bellicose

BEL-ih-kohs, adjective

Demonstrating aggression and a willingness to fight.

“The senator’s bellicose rhetoric alarmed allies abroad.”

KAL-oh, adjective

Inexperienced and immature.

“The capricious weather turned a beach day into a scramble for shelter.”

KAW-stik, adjective

Bitingly sarcastic; able to corrode.

“His caustic reviews were feared, quoted, and secretly enjoyed.”

Excessively talkative.

“The garrulous cab driver covered politics, soup, and his cousin’s wedding in one ride.”

in-TRAN-si-juhnt, adjective

Refusing to compromise.

“Both sides stayed intransigent until the deadline concentrated minds.”

lugubrious

luh-GOO-bree-uhs, adjective

Looking or sounding sad and dismal, often to an exaggerated degree.

“The basset hound’s lugubrious expression suggested a life of quiet tragedy.”

MAWD-lin, adjective

Excessively sentimental.

“The toast turned maudlin after the second glass.”

mendacious

men-DAY-shuhs, adjective

Habitually lying.

“The memoir was entertaining, mendacious, and a bestseller.”

obsequious

uhb-SEE-kwee-uhs, adjective

Excessively eager to please or obey.

“The obsequious waiter refilled glasses no one had touched.”

Stubbornly resistant to authority, direction, or control.

“The recalcitrant printer ignored every command until it was unplugged.”

sahr-DON-ik, adjective

Grimly mocking or cynical.

Silly and pointless without knowing it.

“The panel’s most fatuous question got the longest answer.”

obstreperous

uhb-STREP-er-uhs, adjective

rih-BAR-buh-tiv, adjective

“The form’s rebarbative layout defeats most applicants by page two.”

Gloomy and brooding in temperament.

“The saturnine doorman never once returned a smile.”
